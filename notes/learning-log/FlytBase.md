
**Chapter 1: R&D (FlytBase)**

**Company research**

1. **Company and market analysis**
    
    1. **Core products:** FlytBase sells software, not drones. It flies docked drones on a schedule and runs many sites from one screen. FlytBase One links in cameras and alarms, and its AI agents spot problems in the video.
    2. **Revenue model:** Customers pay a yearly fee per dock, about $4,000–4,500 on Enterprise, plus a $999/year Pro plan for small teams, add-ons and managed services. Revenue grows as a customer adds docks.
    3. **Geographical presence:** It's run from Pune and San Jose, claims 300+ deployments in 40+ countries, and sells through 127 resellers and integrators worldwide.
    4. **Technology stack:** It works with any brand of hardware, though mostly DJI docks. It connects to CCTV software (Genetec, Milestone), mapping tools and SCADA, and can run in the cloud or on the customer's own servers.
2. **Market landscape**
    
    1. **TAM, SAM, SOM:** Drone-in-a-box is a $1.4–1.5B market growing about 20% a year. The software slice FlytBase can serve is roughly $300–500M (my estimate). The near-term SOM is a few thousand energy, mining and security accounts.
    2. **Tapped vs untapped:** Energy, mining and security companies are already customers. Ports, data centres and US police are mostly untouched, either because there's no proof yet or because rivals dominate.
    3. **Buyer journey:** A company starts with manual drones, pilots one or two docks, gets flying permits, then hits the "scale to every site" moment. That last step is where FlytBase wins.
    4. **Seasonality and regulation:** Budgets get set in Q3–Q4, and storm and wildfire seasons raise urgency. The pending US BVLOS rule (Part 108) and the US block on new foreign drones decide how fast the US can buy.
3. **Value proposition analysis**
    
    1. **Strongest value story:** It replaces costly "go and look" trips with a drone already on site. Shell's offshore crew trips cost up to about $20,000 each, which shows what's at stake.
    2. **Pain and value by segment:** Grid operators get fewer crew trips, security teams see an alarm before sending anyone, mines get safer blast zones, and security firms cover more sites with fewer guards.
    3. **Funnel it optimizes:** Land with one to three docks, then expand to many sites, because the fee grows with every dock. Going from 5 docks to 100 means 20× the revenue.
    4. **Competitive advantage:** It's brand-neutral, cheaper than all-in-one rivals, connects to the security control room, and can keep data on the customer's own servers.
4. **Competitive intelligence**
    
    1. **Direct competitors:** DJI FlightHub 2 is cheap but only works with DJI. Percepto and Skydio sell dock, drone and software together at a higher price. Flock and BRINC own US police.
    2. **Indirect competitors:** Guards, CCTV, helicopters and manual drone pilots. Buyers know and trust them, so these are the biggest threat to the budget.
    3. **Gaps and whitespace:** US companies that need software for any brand of drone, ports with few case studies, and security firms in new regions that could resell docks.
    4. **Win/loss:** FlytBase tends to win with multi-site, mixed-brand, integration-heavy buyers outside the US. It tends to lose single-site DJI users and US government buyers who need US-made hardware.
5. **GTM challenges**
    
    1. **Sales cycle:** Grid and oil companies take 6–18 months, because of permits, IT security review and procurement.
    2. **Buyer education:** The market is still early, and many companies need convincing that docks beat manual pilots.
    3. **Channel conflict:** Resellers also sell DJI's own software, and integrators often own the customer relationship, so direct outreach can step on partners.
    4. **No standard buyer:** The drone program sits in security, operations, aviation or innovation depending on the company. This is the problem the assignment asks me to solve.

**Product evaluation**

1. **Saturated with outreach?** Mostly no. Few vendors pitch grid and mining companies on dock software. US police is the exception, where Skydio, Flock and BRINC pitch hard.
2. **Demand capture or demand generation?** Demand capture. The software only matters once a company owns docks, so I target companies already flying drones instead of trying to create the need.
3. **How differentiated?** Strongly for multi-site, mixed-brand programs and weakly against DJI's software at a single site. So outbound should sell the move from a few docks to many.

**Market evaluation**

1. **Saturated?** For grid operators, no. Europe has only about 40 transmission operators, and their inboxes aren't flooded with drone-software pitches.
2. **So what?** I can lean on strong proof like Statnett and Shell and use slightly longer, researched emails. Every account matters, so each one gets researched rather than mass-emailed.

**ICP definition**

### ICP #1: Grid operators (in your Chapter 1 format)

1. **Team structure:** A field operations team, an asset inspection crew, a physical security unit, and ideally an aviation, drone or innovation team. Without people on the ground, nobody can run the docks.
2. **Buying signals:** A drone, UAS or aviation job post, a drone or dock project in the news, a substation break-in or sabotage, staff speaking at drone events, or storm season coming.
3. **Decision-maker profile:** No fixed title. You find them by what they’re measured on: the drone lead (aviation or innovation), the budget owner (grid operations or asset management), and the security owner (physical security). Using DJI docks or Milestone cameras confirms they own the work.
4. **Common pain points:** Paying crews to drive out just to look, slow eyes-on when an alarm goes off at a remote substation, and being stuck at one or two docks with no way to scale.
5. **Past client lookalikes:** Statnett and Xcel Energy. Both are large, with hundreds of unmanned sites, long power lines and an existing drone program. So look for companies with more than 1,000 staff, many substations, and a drone signal.
